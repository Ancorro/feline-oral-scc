Feline Oral Squamous Cell Carcinoma (SCC)

Oral squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) is a cancer of the lining of the mouth and the most common oral tumor in cats (70–80%). This page collects published survival numbers for every treatment, explains each active clinical trial and experimental therapy, and links every figure to its study.

Notes from published studies, not veterinary advice. Most studies are small and look back at past cases. Tumor site, size and the cat's health change the picture; take this to a veterinary oncologist.

  • What works best: full surgical removal of a small front-of-jaw tumor, or radiation that shrinks the tumor completely. Those cats can live 1–2+ years.
  • Standard non-surgical option: accelerated radiation (many sessions in 1–2 weeks) with carboplatin, median about 5–6 months.
  • Chemotherapy (chemo) alone: little benefit from any drug.
  • Low-risk options: pain control, meloxicam, a feeding tube, toceranib and bisphosphonates. They buy comfort and some weeks to months.
  • Experimental: a STAT3-blocking drug showed the first measurable benefit of a targeted drug (35% disease control, 20 cats). Five trials are listed as open.

Radiation therapy

Radiation aims high-energy beams at the tumor over several sessions, each under brief anesthesia. Protocols differ in dose per session (Gy, the unit of absorbed dose) and number of sessions. Courses that pack many sessions into 1–2 weeks ("accelerated") outperform once-a-week palliative courses.

CR = complete response (tumor gone on exam/imaging), PR = partial response (shrank at least 30%).

What predicts a good outcome

  • Small tumor (T1, under 2 cm): 590 d before regrowth vs 105 d overall. [4]
  • Complete response by 30 days: 379 d vs 115 d. [5]
  • Site: tonsil and cheek best; tongue and upper jaw middle; lower jaw worst. [5][8]

Side effects

Mouth inflammation (mucositis) is nearly universal and painful for 2–4 weeks; most clinics place a feeding tube first. Late effects: white fur, skin scarring, and in a minority, bone death and jaw fractures (9 of 47 cats in one series). [3][4]

Chemotherapy

Given alone, no chemotherapy drug has worked well for this tumor. Carboplatin given with radiation is the one combination with decent data.

Without treatment cats usually stop eating within 1–2 months. With pain medicine and supportive care alone, comfortable time is typically 2–3 months. [1]

Surgery

Surgery offers the longest survival when the tumor can be removed with clean margins, mainly at the front of the lower jaw. Tongue, throat and back-of-mouth tumors are rarely removable.

Active clinical trials, explained

Listed as enrolling on institution or registry pages as of October 2026. None confirmed by phone; call before planning around one. Each card says what the treatment is, why researchers think it might work, and what the trial costs the owner.

Personalized RNA vaccine · University of Florida

standard care allowedGainesville, FL

What it is: messenger RNA (mRNA) copied from the cat's own tumor, wrapped in layered lipid nanoparticles, infused into a vein. The particles teach the immune system what the tumor looks like so it attacks it. Same platform that produced strong immune responses against brain tumors in 10 dogs and 4 people.

Protocol
3 IV infusions (30 min) over 7 weeks; rechecks at 2 weeks, 1 and 3 months
Eligible
Any cat with oral SCC and no other major illness. Surgery, radiation or chemo may run alongside
Cost
Study pays vaccine visits, bloodwork, immune monitoring, rechecks. Owner pays diagnostics and standard treatment
Data so far
None published in cats. "Limited enrollment" on UF's list
Contact
Inquiry form on the trial page; PI Dr. Carlos Souza

Refs [14][27]

Injected nanoparticle chemo · University of Illinois

no prior chemo/RTUrbana, IL

What it is: exatecan (a potent chemo drug) packed in sticky nanoparticles injected straight into the tumor. The particles cling to tumor tissue so the drug stays local instead of circulating, aiming for tumor kill without body-wide side effects.

Protocol
Injection under anesthesia every 3 weeks, up to 4; MRI and biopsies at set points; 84-day study
Eligible
Cats over 1 year, mostly soft-tissue oral SCC (under tongue, lip, gum preferred), confirmed by biopsy or cytology, otherwise healthy. No prior chemo, radiation or immunotherapy
Cost
Study pays feeding tube, injections, MRI, biopsies, recheck bloodwork; owner pays supportive meds
Data so far
None published in cats
Contact
Rebecca Kamerer, (217) 300-6453, rmoss81@illinois.edu

Ref [15]

Radiation + camonsertib · NC State

free, ~$8,500 valueRaleigh, NC

What it is: standard palliative radiation (3 sessions) for all cats; some also get camonsertib, a pill that blocks ATR, an enzyme tumors use to repair radiation-damaged DNA. Blocking repair should make the same radiation dose kill more tumor. In human trials with radiation, the main side effect is anemia.

Protocol
1 screening visit, 3 radiation sessions, rechecks at 1 and 2 weeks then monthly
Eligible
Biopsy-confirmed oral SCC anywhere in the mouth, otherwise healthy, no prior chemo or radiation
Cost
All testing and treatment free
Data so far
None published in cats. Registry listing VCT24005841 (Sept 2024)
Contact
Dr. Effie Palyvou, epalyvo@ncsu.edu

Refs [24][28][29]

Intratumoral immunotherapy · Colorado State

Fort Collins, CO

What it is: an unnamed immunotherapy injected directly into the tumor under anesthesia, meant to wake the immune system up inside the tumor. Investigator Steve Dow's lab works on immune-activating injectables. Tumor biopsies before and during treatment track the immune response.

Protocol
4 injections, one every 3 weeks; CT scans before, at visit 3 and 3 weeks after the last; biopsies and blood draws; ends at day 84
Eligible
Not posted; most families live within ~150 miles
Cost
Owner pays diagnosis and any extra tests the oncologist recommends; trial coverage not posted
Data so far
None published
Contact
CSU clinical trials, (970) 297-4001

Lattice radiation · University of Missouri

Columbia, MOlisted 2023, status unknown

What it is: a form of stereotactic radiation that delivers very high dose to a grid of spots inside the tumor and lower dose between them, in only a few sessions. The idea is to kill tumor cores while sparing nearby tissue and triggering an immune response against the rest.

Eligible
Confirmed oral SCC up to 3.5 cm, FeLV/FIV negative, no distant spread, no other cancer, no surgery within 2 weeks
Data so far
None published. PI Charlie Maitz

Ref [26]

Short radiation + olaparib + bexagliflozin · Morris Animal Foundation grant

lab phase first

What it is: a shortened radiation course plus two repurposed drugs: olaparib (a PARP inhibitor, blocks another DNA-repair enzyme) and bexagliflozin (a diabetes drug that starves cells of glucose). Both are meant to make radiation hit harder with fewer anesthesia episodes.

Plan
Cell-line testing first, then 18 pet cats, all also getting standard care
Data so far
None; institution not named on the funder page

Experimental treatments and current data

Treatments that have been tried in cats or cat tumor cells but are not available in regular practice. Ordered from most to least clinical evidence.

STAT3 decoy (CS3D)A small loop of DNA that mimics the sequence the STAT3 protein binds, soaking it up. STAT3 drives tumor growth and suppresses immunity; it has resisted drugs for decades. Target sequence is identical in cats and humans. STAT3 decoy trial in detail (Grandis et al., Cancer Cell 2025) [17]

Design

Where
UC Davis Veterinary Medical Teaching Hospital, with UCSF
Cats
20 client-owned, biopsy-confirmed oral SCC (tonsil excluded), at least 1 year old, 2 kg, tumor 10 mm or larger. Median tumor 2.3 cm (1.1–8.7)
Dosing
IV on days 1, 3, 5, 8, 15, 22 (6 doses). 3 cats each at 2.5, 5, 7.5 mg/kg; 11 cats at 10 mg/kg. Study period 29 days
Allowed
NSAIDs and palliative drugs. No chemo, radiation or bisphosphonates during the study

Results

Response
2 partial responses (one at 5 mg/kg, one at 10 mg/kg), 5 stable, 13 progressed; 6 of the 13 left early
Survival
All 20: median 72.5 d (7–316). Responders: 161 d (73–316). Non-responders: 57 d (7–155), P = 0.004. Survival was not a primary endpoint
Safety
No dose-limiting toxicity up to 10 mg/kg. Anemia in 12 cats (7 grade 1, 5 grade 2), none needing treatment. One responder had skin ulceration over the shrinking tumor, healed by day 22. Rats tolerated 500 mg/kg
On-target
Bcl-XL (a STAT3 target protein) fell in tumors of responders and non-responders alike; tumor-cell death rose only in responders

What predicted response

Blood
Lower neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio before and after treatment (P = 0.028, 0.014); fewer proliferating regulatory T-cells; fewer activated suppressor monocytes
Tumor
Immune-activation genes higher at baseline in responders; PD-1 rose by day 8 in responders, which the authors say supports pairing CS3D with anti-PD-1 drugs
Surprise
Responders had lower baseline activated STAT3 in the tumor, the opposite of what was expected

Caveats and next steps

Limits
Small, single-arm, 29-day window; in mice tumors regrew once dosing stopped, so continued treatment is likely needed
Combos
In mice CS3D plus cetuximab (an EGFR antibody) shrank tumors more than either alone
Interests
Two authors co-invented CS3D and hold interests in Bluedot Bio LLC, the licensee
Next
Authors call for trials in companion animals and human head and neck cancer; no open cat enrollment found as of Oct 2026
