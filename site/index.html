Feline Oral Squamous Cell Carcinoma (SCC)

Oral squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) is a cancer of the lining of the mouth. It makes up 70–80% of cat oral tumors. This page collects the published survival numbers for radiation, chemotherapy, surgery and low-risk comfort care, plus open trials.

Notes from published studies, not veterinary advice. Most studies are small and look back at past cases. Each cat's tumor site, size and health change the picture, so take these to a veterinary oncologist.

The short version

  • Untreated: median survival (MST, the time by which half the cats have died) is about 30–60 days. Fewer than 10% of cats live a year. [1][2]
  • Best results: small tumors that can be fully removed (front of the lower jaw) or that respond completely to radiation. Some of these cats live 1–2+ years. [2][4][6]
  • Radiation: the strongest non-surgical option. Accelerated courses give a median of ~5–6 months and 29% alive at one year. [4][5]
  • Chemotherapy alone: does little. Carboplatin helps mainly as a booster given with radiation. [1][5][10]
  • Low-risk options: pain control, meloxicam, a feeding tube, toceranib (an oral pill) and bisphosphonates. They mainly buy comfort and some time. [1][2][9]

Recent data, 2025–2026

New since 2025. No new treatment has changed the standard outlook yet. The first targeted-drug trial in cats showed some activity, and genetic work points at different targets than the ones tried so far.

Radiation therapy

Radiation aims high-energy beams at the tumor over several sessions, each under brief anesthesia. Protocols differ in dose per session (Gy, the unit of absorbed dose) and how many sessions. Courses that use many sessions in a short time do better than once-a-week palliative courses.

What predicts a good radiation outcome

  • Small tumor: T1 tumors (under 2 cm) did far better in the 4.8 Gy × 10 study (590 d without progression). [4]
  • Complete response by 30 days: 379 d vs 115 d for cats without one. [5]
  • Site: tonsil and cheek best; tongue 175 d, upper jaw 160 d, lower jaw 130 d with radiation + carboplatin. [5]

Side effects

Mouth inflammation (mucositis) is nearly universal and painful for a few weeks. Many clinics place a feeding tube ahead of time. Late effects include white fur, scarring, and in a minority, bone death and jaw fractures (9 of 47 cats in one series). [3][4]

Chemotherapy

Given alone, no chemotherapy drug has worked well for this tumor. Carboplatin given with radiation is the one combination with decent data.

Low-risk and comfort-focused care

These need little or no anesthesia and can run alongside other treatment or on their own. They mostly improve comfort; a few modestly extend survival.

Without treatment cats usually stop eating within 1–2 months. With pain medicine and supportive care alone, comfortable time is typically 2–3 months.

Surgery

Surgery offers the longest survival when the tumor can be removed with clean margins, mainly at the front of the lower jaw. Removing part of the jaw (mandibulectomy) gave an MST of 217 d overall and 911 d for front-of-jaw tumors, with 38% recurrence. Most cats have trouble eating afterwards for a while; 41% needed a feeding tube. Tongue, throat and back-of-mouth tumors are rarely removable. [1][6]

Clinical trials and new treatments

  • University of Florida, RNA nanoparticle vaccine. Any cat with oral SCC and no other major illness; standard treatment allowed alongside. Study pays for 3 IV vaccine visits over 7 weeks and rechecks. PI (principal investigator): Dr. Carlos Souza. Trial page. [14]
  • University of Illinois, injected nanoparticle chemotherapy (exatecan). Cats over 1 year with soft-tissue oral SCC (under the tongue, lip or gum preferred), no prior chemo, radiation or immunotherapy. Up to 4 injections; study covers feeding tube, MRI and biopsies. Contact: Rebecca Kamerer, (217) 300-6453. Flyer (Nov 2025). [15]
  • STAT3 decoy (UCSF/UC Davis), phase 1, 20 cats. Little toxicity; disease stopped growing in 5 cats and shrank in 2. Further cat trials planned; no open enrollment found. Coverage. [16]
  • Lab-stage: EGFR (epidermal growth factor receptor) blockers such as cetuximab, checkpoint immunotherapy, photodynamic therapy. Not yet available to cats. [2]

Enrollment status not confirmed by phone; call before planning around a trial.
