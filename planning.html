Start here

Oral squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) kills by growing where it is, not by spreading: in 49 advanced cases, 41 cats died of the tumor in the mouth. So the first question is whether the tumor can be removed or controlled locally, and that depends on three things a general practice usually cannot tell you: exactly how far it extends into bone, whether the jaw lymph nodes are involved, and whether the cat can take anesthesia. [62]

Three things to establish before choosing

  1. A head CT at the hospital that would treat. The visible lump underestimates the tumor; the CT decides whether surgery or radiation is possible and is used to plan either. [1, 36]
  2. Staging. A needle sample of both jaw lymph nodes whether or not they feel enlarged, chest X-rays or a chest CT, and blood and urine tests. [1]
  3. Anesthetic fitness. Every surgical and radiation option needs one to fifteen anesthesias; kidney, heart and thyroid status set the limits. [3, 4]

Which situation is the cat in?

SituationHow to recognize itRealistic goal
A. Small tumor a surgeon judges removableUsually under 2 cm and at the front of the lower or upper jaw; no node or lung spread on staging. A minority of cats. [6, 36]A year or more. Median 217 days after lower-jaw surgery (911 days for front-of-jaw tumors); 83% alive at two years after upper-jaw surgery. [6, 36]
B. Not removable, cat fit for repeated anesthesiaTumor too large or too far back to take with a margin, but staging clear and organs sound. [4, 5]Five to six months median with accelerated radiation; much longer if the tumor is small or disappears completely. [4, 5]
C. Comfort firstAdvanced tumor, spread already present, poor anesthetic risk, or an owner who declines hospital courses. Most cats at diagnosis. [1, 61]Two to four months of eating and grooming comfortably; 30 to 60 days without treatment. [1, 9, 59]

Two facts cut across the situations: a small tumor and a complete response to radiation are the strongest predictors of long survival, and site matters (front of the lower jaw, tonsil and cheek do best; under the tongue and back of the lower jaw do worst). [4, 5, 8, 61]

Options by situation

Each entry gives what the cat goes through, what to expect, what it costs, and where it is done. Prices are United States list prices and published 2026 cost surveys, before insurance. [57]

Situation A: surgery

Maxillectomy (part of the upper jaw)Thirteen SCC cats: median survival not reached, 83% alive at one and two years. One operation; poor appetite for about a week; the wound breaks down in 20%; the face is visibly changed. [36]
Mandibulectomy (part of the lower jaw)Twenty-one SCC cats: median 217 days, 43% alive at one year, 911 days for front-of-jaw tumors. One operation; 41% needed a feeding tube, 12% never ate on their own again; 83% of owners were satisfied. [6]
Radical optionsRemoving 75 to 90% of the lower jaw (8 cats, mean 712 days) or the whole lower jaw and tongue (20 cats, median 533 days) gives the longest survivals in the literature at the cost of permanent tube feeding and daily home care. [2, 63]
What decides itA clean margin: after lower-jaw surgery the tumor returned in 9 of 16 cats with incomplete margins and 4 of 17 with clean ones. Plan a bone margin of about 1 cm from the CT. If margins come back incomplete, ask about radiation to the site. [6, 36]
Cost and whereA referral-level operation at a specialty or university surgical service; price is quoted after the CT. Specialty surgery in cats commonly runs $3,000 to $8,000 including imaging and hospitalization; ask for the itemized estimate. [57]

Situation B: radiation

Accelerated radiation, 4.8 Gy in each of 10 daily sessionsTwenty-one cats: median 174 days, 29% alive at one year; small (T1) tumors went 590 days before regrowing. Ten anesthesias over two weeks; mouth sores for two to four weeks; a feeding tube is usual. [4]
Accelerated radiation with carboplatinTwo series of 31 and 47 cats: median 163 to 164 days, about 15% alive at one year; cats whose tumor disappeared lived a median of 379 days; tonsil and cheek tumors did best. Fourteen or fifteen anesthesias over nine to ten days plus two chemotherapy doses; 78% needed a feeding tube; about one in five later had a jaw fracture. Developed and still run at Washington State University. [3, 5]
What to expectPainful mouth inflammation for two to four weeks is nearly universal; most clinics place the feeding tube before starting. Later: white fur, skin scarring, and in a minority bone death and jaw fractures. [3, 4]
What not to addToceranib after radiation did not lengthen survival (208 versus 162 days), except that tongue tumors regrew later. Gemcitabine and the bleomycin-piroxicam-thalidomide combination added toxicity without benefit. [3]
Cost and whereOnly at hospitals with a linear accelerator: in Oregon and Washington that means Clackamas and Pullman. A full curative course is $5,000 to $10,000 at 2026 survey prices, plus a planning CT ($800 to $2,500) and consultation ($150 to $300). [57, 80, 82]

Situation C: comfort first

Weekly palliative radiationThree or four weekly anesthesias; about half of cats get relief; median two to four months (113 days alone in a 54-cat series). A course is $1,000 to $3,000, up to $5,000 at some specialty networks. [8, 57]
Toceranib tablets with meloxicamForty-six cats: median 123 days against 45 without, 20% alive at one year; most of the "responses" were stable disease, and NSAID use confounds the result. A tablet at home three days a week; about $115 a month for a 4 to 5 kg cat plus monitoring bloodwork. [9, 55]
Pain control, meloxicam and a feeding tubeThe universal baseline, covered on the Comfort care page. About two to three months of comfortable time from general experience. [1, 38]
Zoledronate if the tumor is in boneNine cats with weekly radiation: 44% partial response, median 136 days, mild side effects. A 15-minute infusion about monthly; the drug is cheap if the clinic splits vials, the visit $150 to $400. [25, 56]

Comfort care in every plan

Whichever situation the cat is in, these run alongside. Pain is the usual reason a cat stops eating and grooming. All drugs need a veterinary prescription. [38]

Pain reliefBuprenorphine by mouth every eight hours (controlled; from the clinic or a compounding pharmacy, roughly $20 to $40 for a few days), gabapentin as an add-on ($35 to $79 for compounded liquid at Chewy), antibiotics when the tumor surface is infected. [38, 47, 49]
MeloxicamThe one medicine with a consistent survival association: an NSAID after diagnosis halved the risk of death at any time in 54 general-practice cats (hazard ratio 0.46), and every one-year survivor in the toceranib study had one. Low daily doses have not sped up kidney disease in older cats in long-term studies; kidney values are checked every few months. Not with steroids. About $31 to $41 a bottle, which lasts months. [59, 9, 40, 41, 50]
Feeding tubeA tube through the neck into the esophagus, placed under one short anesthesia ($500 to $800). Food, water and medicine bypass the mouth. In 248 cats, 36% had a complication, mostly the tube coming out or a skin infection; none died of it; 96% of owners were comfortable managing one. It should be placed before the cat stops eating, and the main practice sources advise against using it to prolong life once the tumor itself prevents eating. [38, 42, 52, 69]
AppetiteMirtazapine ointment in the ear once daily: 3.9% weight gain in two weeks against 0.4% on placebo in 177 cats. $53 a tube, less on autoship. [43, 54]

Where to go in the Pacific Northwest

Radiation for pets exists at two sites in Oregon and Washington combined. Medical oncologists (chemotherapy, toceranib, comfort care, referral for radiation) are in Portland, Vancouver, Seattle, Tacoma, Bellevue and Spokane. [81]

Washington State University, PullmanThe hospital that developed the accelerated radiation plus carboplatin protocol; its radiation oncologist is senior author of both outcome series. New linear accelerator since 2023; lists feline oral SCC among cancers treated. No referral needed for a second opinion. 509-335-0711. About 390 miles from Corvallis. [3, 5, 82, 83, 84]
VCA Northwest Veterinary Specialists, ClackamasIntensity-modulated, stereotactic and palliative radiation for cats under a board-certified radiation oncologist. Referral through the primary veterinarian. 503-656-3999. About 85 miles from Corvallis. [80]
Pacific Northwest Pet ER and Specialty Center, Vancouver, WAMedical oncologist whose residency research included feline oral SCC; chemotherapy, targeted drugs, palliative care; 10 miles from the Clackamas radiation unit. 360-635-5302. [85, 89]
Seattle areaMedical oncology at Veterinary Specialty Center of Seattle in Lynnwood (425-697-6106), Bellevue Veterinary Emergency and Specialty, Animal Medical Center of Seattle, and Summit in Tacoma (253-983-1114). A stereotactic radiation unit was announced for Lynnwood for early 2026 and is not yet confirmed open; call. [85, 86, 87]
SpokanePet Emergency Clinic, referral-only medical oncology, 509-326-7246; radiation 75 miles away at WSU. [90]

Trials worth a phone call

No trial for this cancer exists west of Colorado, and every trial requires repeated visits at its hospital. These are the ones listed as open in October 2026 that a committed owner could consider; none is confirmed by phone. [22, 37]

Colorado State University, Fort Collins: immunotherapy injected into the tumor

Registry listing →

Four injections under anesthesia, one every three weeks, with CT scans and biopsies. Needs a reachable tumor, no spread beyond local nodes, sound kidneys and liver. Owner pays diagnosis; families are expected to live within about 150 miles or relocate. Recruiting August 2026 to December 2027. csuoncologytrials@colostate.edu, 970-297-4001. [22, 37]

University of Illinois, Urbana: three-day radiation with olaparib and bexagliflozin

Trial flyer (PDF) →

Three consecutive daily radiation sessions plus two tablets at home; the trial pays for radiation, the feeding tube and the drugs. Excludes diabetes and kidney disease at IRIS stage 2 or worse. 18 cats planned. Rebecca Kamerer, 217-300-6453. [70]

University of Pennsylvania, Philadelphia: pain study with palliative radiation

Trial page →

The study pays for six palliative radiation sessions, meloxicam and the trial antibody or placebo; the owner pays initial staging. For jaw tumors with bone destruction on CT and no spread. Six visits over 56 days. 215-573-0302. [71]

Questions to ask the oncologist

  1. On the CT, can this tumor be removed with about a 1 cm bone margin, and if so which procedure? What did the lymph node samples and chest imaging show? [1, 36]
  2. If surgery is possible, what is the expected eating recovery for this procedure, how likely is a feeding tube, and for how long? [6, 36]
  3. If not, is the tumor small enough (under 2 cm) that a curative-intent radiation course is justified, and which protocol does this hospital use? [4, 5]
  4. How many anesthesias will the plan need, and is this cat's kidney, heart and thyroid status good enough for them? [3, 4]
  5. Will a feeding tube be placed before treatment starts, and who teaches me to use it? [38, 42]
  6. What pain plan starts today, and can meloxicam be used given the kidney values? [38, 40]
  7. What is the itemized cost of the plan, and what does the first month of comfort-only care cost by comparison? [57]
  8. At what point would you consider the cat's quality of life no longer acceptable, and how will we recognize it? [38]
