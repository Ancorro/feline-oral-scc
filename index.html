Oral squamous cell carcinoma in cats

Overview

Oral squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) is a cancer of the lining of the mouth and the most common oral tumor in cats, about 70–80% of cases. Untreated, median survival is 30–60 days and fewer than 10% of cats live a year. [1, 2]

The longest survival comes from two situations: a small tumor at the front of the lower jaw that can be fully removed, or a tumor that disappears completely after radiation. Those cats can live one to two years or more. [4, 5, 6]

For everything else, the standard non-surgical option is an accelerated course of radiation with carboplatin, which gives a median of about five to six months. Chemotherapy alone does little. Comfort care (pain control, an anti-inflammatory, a feeding tube, the pill toceranib) buys weeks to months and keeps the cat eating. [3, 5, 9]

Experimentally, a STAT3-blocking drug produced the first measurable benefit of a targeted drug in this cancer, and several trials are listed as open. [17]

Radiation

Radiation aims high-energy beams at the tumor over several sessions, each under brief anesthesia. Protocols differ in dose per session (Gy, the unit of absorbed dose) and number of sessions. Courses that pack many sessions into one to two weeks ("accelerated") do better than once-a-week palliative courses.

What predicts a good outcome

  • A small tumor, under 2 cm: 590 days before regrowth versus 105 overall. [4]
  • Complete disappearance by 30 days: 379 days versus 115. [5]
  • Site: tonsil and cheek best, tongue and upper jaw in the middle, lower jaw worst. [5, 8]

Side effects

Mouth inflammation is nearly universal and painful for two to four weeks; most clinics place a feeding tube first. Later effects include white fur and skin scarring, and in a minority bone death and jaw fractures (9 of 47 cats in one series). [3, 4]

Chemotherapy

Given alone, no chemotherapy drug has worked well for this tumor. Carboplatin given alongside radiation is the one combination with decent data.

Low-risk care

These need little or no anesthesia and can run alongside other treatment or on their own. They mainly improve comfort; a few modestly extend survival. Without treatment cats usually stop eating within one to two months; with pain medicine and supportive care, comfortable time is typically two to three months. [1]

  • Pain control. Oral tumors hurt; eating and grooming improve when pain is managed. [1]
  • Meloxicam, an anti-inflammatory (NSAID). Pain relief and possibly a mild anti-tumor effect; preferred over piroxicam in cats. NSAID use was linked to longer survival in the toceranib study. Kidneys need monitoring. [1, 9]
  • Esophagostomy feeding tube. Food, water and pills without chewing. One short anesthesia to place; routine with radiation and after jaw surgery. [1, 3]
  • Toceranib, a pill given at home three days a week, usually with meloxicam. Median 123 versus 45 days; 20% alive at one year; mild stomach and blood-count effects. [9]
  • Zoledronate or pamidronate (bisphosphonates). Slow the tumor's bone destruction and ease bone pain. With weekly radiation, 44% partial response and median 136 days. [2, 25]
  • Palliative radiation, three or four weekly sessions. About half of cats get relief; median two to four months. More involved than the others. [1, 8]

Surgery

Surgery offers the longest survival when the tumor can be removed with clean margins, mainly at the front of the lower jaw. Tongue, throat and back-of-mouth tumors are rarely removable.

  • Mandibulectomy (part of the lower jaw removed), 21 cats with SCC. Median 217 days; 43% alive at one and two years; front-of-jaw tumors 911 days; 38% recurrence. Afterwards 72% had trouble eating at first, 12% never ate on their own again and 41% needed a feeding tube; 83% of owners were satisfied. [6]
  • Radical mandibulectomy (75–90% of the jaw), 8 cats. Mean 712 days; 3 recurrences; 6 of 8 ate on their own. [2]
  • Maxillectomy (part of the upper jaw removed), 60 cats at 14 hospitals, 13 of them with SCC. Across all 60, median survival was not reached, 18% had local recurrence, and the commonest complications were poor appetite (20%, median 7 days) and wound breakdown (20%). For the 13 SCC cats, 83% were alive at one and two years; 2 of 13 had incomplete removal and 1 died of recurrence. Selection matters: these were tumors a surgeon judged removable, often small (median 2 cm), and 8 of the 60 cats also received chemotherapy. [36]

Clinical trials

Listed as enrolling on institution or registry pages as of October 2026. None confirmed by phone; call before planning around one.

Personalized RNA vaccine, University of Florida

Messenger RNA copied from the cat's own tumor, wrapped in lipid nanoparticles and infused into a vein, to teach the immune system what the tumor looks like. The same platform produced strong immune responses against brain tumors in dogs and people. Three 30-minute infusions over seven weeks, then rechecks at two weeks, one month and three months. Any cat with oral SCC and no other major illness is eligible, and surgery, radiation or chemotherapy may run alongside. The study pays for the vaccine visits, bloodwork and rechecks; the owner pays for diagnostics and standard treatment. No cat results published. Contact through the trial page; PI Dr. Carlos Souza. [14, 27]

Injected nanoparticle chemotherapy, University of Illinois

Exatecan, a potent chemotherapy drug, packed in sticky nanoparticles and injected into the tumor under anesthesia, every three weeks up to four times, so the drug stays local. Cats must be over one year old with mostly soft-tissue oral SCC (under the tongue, lip or gum preferred) and no prior chemotherapy, radiation or immunotherapy. The study pays for the feeding tube, injections, MRI, biopsies and recheck bloodwork; the owner pays for supportive medicines. No cat results published. Contact Rebecca Kamerer, (217) 300-6453, rmoss81@illinois.edu. [15]

Radiation plus camonsertib, NC State

All cats get three sessions of standard palliative radiation; some also get camonsertib, a pill that blocks ATR, an enzyme tumors use to repair radiation damage, so the same dose should kill more tumor. In human trials the main side effect is anemia. Requires biopsy-confirmed oral SCC, otherwise good health and no prior chemotherapy or radiation. All testing and treatment are free (about $8,500 value). Flyer dated September 2024. Contact Dr. Effie Palyvou, epalyvo@ncsu.edu. [24, 28, 29]

Intratumoral immunotherapy (LTN), Colorado State

Lipid Toll-like-receptor nanoparticles (LTN), an agent that switches on the innate immune system, injected directly into the tumor under anesthesia four times at three-week intervals, with CT scans and biopsies before, at the third injection and three weeks after the fourth. The approach comes from two trials in horses with SCC that CSU reports as highly successful; in cats it has been tested in only one oral SCC case so far. Stopping tumor growth, not just shrinkage, counts as success. Eligible: confirmed oral SCC of the gum, tongue, lip or jaw bone that can be reached with a needle, no spread beyond local lymph nodes, good liver and kidney function, fit for five anesthesias. Excluded: ear or nose tumors, advanced kidney disease, liver disease, diabetes, or immune-suppressing drugs. Expected side effects: mild swelling at the injection site, a day or so of tiredness and reduced appetite. Recruiting August 2026 to December 2027; all visits in Fort Collins; the owner pays for diagnosis. Investigator Steve Dow. Contact csuoncologytrials@colostate.edu, (970) 297-4001. Registry VCT26005965. [22, 37]

Lattice radiation, University of Missouri

A form of stereotactic radiation that gives a very high dose to a grid of spots inside the tumor in a few sessions, aiming to kill tumor cores while sparing nearby tissue and provoking an immune response. Requires confirmed oral SCC up to 3.5 cm, negative FeLV and FIV tests and no distant spread. Listed in 2023; current status unknown. PI Charlie Maitz. [26]

Short radiation with olaparib and bexagliflozin, Morris Animal Foundation grant

A shortened radiation course plus two repurposed drugs: olaparib (blocks a DNA-repair enzyme) and bexagliflozin (a diabetes drug that starves cells of glucose). Cell testing comes first, then 18 pet cats who also receive standard care. The institution is not named on the funder page. [30]

Experimental treatments

Treatments tried in cats or cat tumor cells but not available in regular practice, from most to least evidence.

STAT3 decoy (CS3D)

A small loop of DNA that mimics the sequence the STAT3 protein binds, soaking it up. STAT3 drives tumor growth and suppresses immunity and has resisted drugs for decades; the target sequence is identical in cats and humans. In mice the effect depended on CD8 T-cells, so it works partly through the immune system. [17]

A phase 1 trial at UC Davis (Cancer Cell, November 2025) treated 20 cats with six intravenous doses over 22 days at 2.5–10 mg/kg. Two cats had partial responses, five were stable and thirteen progressed: a 35% disease-control rate, with no relation to dose. Median survival was 72.5 days overall; the seven responders lived a median of 161 days versus 57 for the rest (P = 0.004), though survival was not a primary endpoint. The only side effect was mild anemia in 12 cats, none needing treatment. Responders had a lower neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio in the blood, more immune-activation genes in the tumor at baseline, and a rise in PD-1 by day 8, which the authors say supports pairing the drug with anti-PD-1 immunotherapy. Unexpectedly, responders had less activated STAT3 in their tumors to begin with. In mice tumors regrew once dosing stopped. Two authors co-invented the drug and hold interests in the licensee. Further cat and human trials are planned; no open cat enrollment was found. [17]

IB-DNQ with weekly radiation

A drug activated by the enzyme NQO1, which is high in these tumors and rises further after radiation; once activated it floods tumor cells with reactive oxygen. A prospective trial at Illinois gave 18 cats 8 Gy weekly for four weeks plus IB-DNQ: 17% partial response, 78% stable, with no blood or organ toxicity. Survival was not reported and no follow-on trial was found. [31, 32]

Radiation with zoledronate

A bisphosphonate that blocks the bone-eating cells the tumor recruits, given with weekly radiation for bone-invasive tumors. Nine cats: 22–44% partial response depending on the measure, 78% stable, median 136 days, all side effects mild. [25]

Electrochemotherapy

Electric pulses open pores in tumor cells so bleomycin floods in. Routine for skin SCC in Europe (78% response in 23 cats, 2025). In the mouth, one tongue case: three sessions shrank the tumor, eased pain and the cat gained 0.9 kg, but autopsy found deep spread. [18, 21]

Not yet tested in cats

  • EGFR blockers (cetuximab, gefitinib) slowed growth in cell lines, but 2026 DNA sequencing found EGFR amplified in only 6% of tumors, so they are unlikely to work alone. [2, 20]
  • PI3K/mTOR inhibitors: proposed from the same sequencing (PTEN lost in 44% of tumors), ideally paired with carboplatin or radiation. [20]
  • Checkpoint immunotherapy (anti-PD-1): tumors express PD-L1, but mutation burden is low, which predicts a weaker response. An earlier LAK-cell immunotherapy was safe but did not extend survival. [2, 17, 20]
  • Light-activated therapies: EGFR-targeting nanobody photodynamic therapy killed feline SCC cell lines (Utrecht, 2021); porphysome photodynamic therapy is funded at Guelph. No cat treated yet. [33, 34]
  • Lactate-transport blockers: tumor cells feed on lactic acid from neighboring cells; confirmed in cat tumors, but the drug is not available for cats. [35]
  • Tried and abandoned: umbilical-cord stem cells (survival under a month), liposomal cisplatin (no responses), ethanol ablation (transient shrinkage). [2]

Recent data, 2025–2026

  • April 2025. Radiation plus carboplatin, 47 cats: median 164 days. Adding toceranib afterwards made no significant difference (208 versus 162 days), except tongue tumors regrew later. [3]
  • July 2025. Electrochemotherapy for skin SCC, 23 cats: 78% response, early-stage best. [18]
  • October 2025. Review of the field in Frontiers in Veterinary Science. [2]
  • November 2025. STAT3 decoy phase 1 in 20 cats, Cancer Cell: 35% disease control; responders lived 161 versus 57 days; mild anemia only. The first targeted drug with measurable benefit here. [17]
  • January 2026. 4,300 biopsy cases in Germany: 41% of cat SCC in the mouth, 36% on skin. [19]
  • February 2026. DNA sequencing of 36 tumors: TP53 mutated in 71%, PTEN lost in 44%, MYC gained in 41%, EGFR gained in only 6%. The tumors resemble human HPV-negative head and neck cancer. EGFR drugs alone look unlikely to work; PI3K/mTOR drugs with radiation are suggested; checkpoint drugs still worth testing. [20]
  • 2026. Electrochemotherapy for a tongue tumor, one cat: shrank and the cat ate again; deep spread found at autopsy. [21]
