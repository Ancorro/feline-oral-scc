Overview

Oral squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) is a cancer of the lining of the mouth and the most common oral tumor in cats, about 70–80% of cases. Untreated, median survival is 30–60 days and fewer than 10% of cats live a year. [1, 2]

The longest survival comes from two situations: a small tumor at the front of the lower jaw that can be fully removed, or a tumor that disappears completely after radiation. Those cats can live one to two years or more. [4, 5, 6]

For everything else, the standard non-surgical option is an accelerated course of radiation with carboplatin, which gives a median of about five to six months. Chemotherapy alone does little. Comfort care (pain control, an anti-inflammatory, a feeding tube, the pill toceranib) buys weeks to months and keeps the cat eating. [3, 5, 9]

Experimentally, a STAT3-blocking drug produced the first measurable benefit of a targeted drug in this cancer, and several trials are listed as open. [17]

What untreated cats face

Four independent series agree on the baseline. In 54 cats seen in United Kingdom general practice the median survival was 44 days and 9.5% were alive at one year [59]. Twenty-three untreated cats at a New York referral hospital lived a median of 45 days [9]. Sixty-seven archived Scottish cases had a median of 46 days and 5% one-year survival [60]. In a New Zealand series given only comfort care, site mattered: tumors at the back of the throat gave a median of 151 days, while upper-jaw (51 days), under-tongue (33 days) and lower-jaw (34 days) tumors gave about a month [61].

Death comes from the tumor in the mouth, not from spread. In 49 advanced cases at the University of Pennsylvania, 41 cats died or were euthanized because of local growth, even though 31% had cancer in the jaw lymph nodes and 10% had possible lung spread [62]. This is why every treatment that lengthens life does so by controlling the tumor where it sits.

Staging before choosing

Reviews converge on the same work-up: a needle sample of both jaw lymph nodes whether or not they feel enlarged, chest X-rays or a chest CT, a head CT at the treating hospital to plan surgery or radiation, and blood and urine tests. Staging mainly sorts cats into those who can be offered curative-intent treatment and those who cannot [1].

Radiation

Radiation aims high-energy beams at the tumor over several sessions, each under brief anesthesia. Protocols differ in the dose per session (measured in gray, Gy) and in the number of sessions. Courses that pack many sessions into one or two weeks, called accelerated courses, do better than once-a-week palliative courses.

What predicts a good outcome

  • A small tumor, under 2 cm: 590 days before regrowth versus 105 overall. [4]
  • Complete disappearance by 30 days: 379 days versus 115. [5]
  • Site: tonsil and cheek best, tongue and upper jaw in the middle, lower jaw worst. [5, 8]

Side effects

Mouth inflammation is nearly universal and painful for two to four weeks; most clinics place a feeding tube first. Later effects include white fur and skin scarring, and in a minority bone death and jaw fractures (9 of 47 cats in one series). [3, 4]

Chemotherapy

Given alone, no chemotherapy drug has worked well for this tumor; the single-drug series are indistinguishable from untreated cats. Carboplatin given alongside accelerated radiation is the one combination with decent data. Cisplatin, the related drug used in people, is lethal to cats: in the defining toxicity study all four cats given 60 mg per square meter died of lung fluid within two to four days [66].

Low-risk care

These measures need little or no anesthesia and can run alongside radiation, surgery or a trial, or stand on their own. Their main purpose is comfort: keeping the cat eating, grooming and free of pain. A few also extend survival modestly. Without treatment, most cats stop eating within one to two months; with pain medicine and supportive care, comfortable time is typically two to three months. [1, 38]

Prices below are United States list prices from online pharmacies and published cost surveys in October 2026, before any insurance. Every drug on this page needs a veterinary prescription. Online pharmacies such as Chewy, Allivet and VetRxDirect fill prescriptions once the veterinarian approves them; controlled drugs and infusions come only from the clinic.

Pain control

Oral tumors are painful, and pain is the usual reason a cat stops eating and grooming. Pain control is the first step in every plan, whatever else is done. [38]

BuprenorphineAn opioid that owners give at home by mouth, where it absorbs through the gums, typically every eight hours. It is often the first drug prescribed. A Schedule III controlled drug, so it comes from the clinic or a compounding pharmacy such as Wedgewood, not from general online pharmacies. A three-to-five-day supply costs about $20 to $40; owner reports of a full 10 mL bottle range from about $85 to $270 depending on the clinic. Zorbium is a four-day transdermal version applied in the clinic at about $50 to $90 per dose. [38, 39, 47, 48]
GabapentinAdded for nerve-related pain, with a calming effect. Compounded liquid for cats (100 mg/mL) is about $35 for 15 mL to $79 for 120 mL at Chewy; generic capsules are cheaper from a human pharmacy with a prescription. [38, 49]
Fentanyl patchSeveral days of continuous opioid for cats that resist oral dosing; applied and replaced at the clinic. Controlled; priced by the clinic. [39]
AntibioticsAmoxicillin-clavulanate or clindamycin when the tumor surface is ulcerated and infected, which adds to pain and odor. Generic courses are usually under $30 from the clinic or a pharmacy. [39]

Meloxicam

A non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID). It reduces tumor-related inflammation and swelling as well as pain, and NSAIDs may have a mild anti-tumor effect of their own. It is preferred over piroxicam in cats. [1]

EvidenceNSAIDs are the one medicine with a consistent survival association. In 54 general-practice cats, an NSAID after diagnosis roughly halved the risk of death at any time (hazard ratio 0.46, 95% confidence interval 0.21 to 0.98). In the 46-cat toceranib study, NSAID use was tied to 169 versus 55 days (P = 0.004), and every cat alive at one year had received one. Neither study was randomized, so cats well enough to take an NSAID may simply have been healthier; and the drug target, COX-2, is present in only about half of these tumors. [59, 9, 68]
Dose and safetyIn the United States the label allows only a single injection, with a warning about kidney injury from repeated use. Outside the United States, long-term low doses of 0.01 to 0.03 mg/kg once daily have been studied in older cats, including cats with stable kidney disease, without faster progression of kidney disease. A 2022 study found low-dose meloxicam safe and tolerable alongside toceranib in cancer-bearing cats, with kidney values checked regularly. [40, 41]
Practical viewReviewers consider cats with oral SCC good candidates, because the tumor is far more likely to end the cat's life than kidney disease is. It must not be combined with a steroid such as prednisolone, which causes stomach ulcers. [1, 38]
Cost and wherePrescription, off-label for cats in the United States. Metacam oral suspension 1.5 mg/mL, 10 mL, is about $31 with a GoodRx coupon or at Allivet; the 0.5 mg/mL cat strength, 15 mL, is about $41. At cat doses a bottle lasts months. Kidney bloodwork every few months adds $50 to $150 per panel. [50, 51]

Feeding tube

An esophagostomy tube is a soft tube placed through the side of the neck into the esophagus under one short anesthesia. Food, water and medicines go through it, bypassing the painful mouth. One review calls it the single most important measure for quality of life in this disease, and advises placing it before the cat stops eating rather than after. [38]

How commonRoutine with accelerated radiation (78% of cats in one series) and after jaw surgery (41%). [3, 5, 6]
ComplicationsIn 248 cats, 36% had a complication, mostly the tube coming out (15%) or infection at the skin site (12%). None died from the tube, and problems were fixed by cleaning, antibiotics or removal. Cats on chemotherapy or steroids had higher infection odds. Tubes stayed in for a median of 11 days and up to 93. [42]
LimitsOwners manage these tubes well: 96% of 24 surveyed owners were comfortable with the care. But no study shows a tube lengthens life in this disease, and the two main practice sources advise against using one to prolong life once the tumor itself prevents eating, treating that point as the time to discuss euthanasia. [69, 1, 38]
Cost and wherePlaced at a general or specialty clinic. Placement with anesthesia is commonly quoted at $500 to $800; owner-itemized estimates show about $540 for the procedure plus $150 to $300 for bloodwork and anesthesia. The tube itself costs about $10 to $13. Liquid recovery diets run roughly $3 to $5 per can. [52, 53]

Appetite stimulant

Mirtazapine as an ointment rubbed inside the ear once daily (Mirataz) is licensed for weight loss in cats with chronic illness. In a placebo-controlled trial of 177 cats with unintended weight loss, treated cats gained 3.9% of body weight in two weeks against 0.4% on placebo; the main side effect was mild redness at the ear. It helps a cat that can still eat but has lost interest; it does not remove the pain of chewing. [43]

Cost and wherePrescription. A 5 g tube, about a month at the label dose, is $53 at Chewy, Banfield and VetRxDirect, and $37 to $47 with pharmacy autoship discounts. Oral mirtazapine tablets, split to a cat dose, cost a few dollars a month from a human pharmacy. [54]

Toceranib (Palladia)

A tablet that blocks several growth-signal receptors on tumor cells and blood vessels. It is given at home, usually every other day or three days a week, at about 2.5 mg/kg, and is normally combined with meloxicam. [9]

SurvivalIn 46 cats, those given toceranib lived a median of 123 days (range 34 to 419) against 45 days (range 1 to 303) for cats given neither toceranib, chemotherapy nor radiation (P = 0.01). About 20% were alive at one year. Most cats still died of local tumor growth. [9]
Side effectsIn a separate series of 35 cats, 6 (17%) had any toxicity. Across feline toceranib studies the common effects are stomach upset (about 22%), low platelets (16%), rising kidney values (15%) and low white cells (9%). In the 46-cat study, all five cats whose kidney values worsened were on toceranib plus an NSAID, so blood and urine tests are repeated during treatment. [9, 44]
After radiationAdding toceranib after radiation and carboplatin did not significantly lengthen survival (208 versus 162 days), except that tongue tumors regrew later. [3]
Cost and wherePrescription, labeled for dogs and used off-label in cats. Chewy sells the 10 mg tablet at $8.74 and the 15 mg at $12.78, with a 30% first-order discount; Walmart lists 15 mg at $11.94. A 4 to 5 kg cat takes one 10 mg tablet per dose, three times a week, so about 13 tablets and $115 a month, plus monitoring bloodwork of $100 to $200 every month or two at first. [55]

Bisphosphonates

Zoledronate and pamidronate are infusions that block the bone-dissolving cells the tumor recruits when it invades the jaw. In people and dogs they reduce bone-cancer pain. [2, 45]

Zoledronate0.2 mg/kg by a 15-minute intravenous infusion, repeated about every four weeks. In eight cats with bone-invading oral SCC it lowered the blood marker of bone breakdown and was well tolerated with meloxicam. With four weekly radiation sessions in nine cats, 44% had a partial response and median survival was 136 days, with only mild side effects. [25, 46]
PamidronateA two-hour infusion. In eight cats with bone-invasive tumors it was safe and stabilized disease in some, without shrinking tumors. [45]
CaveatNo cat study has measured pain scores directly, so the pain benefit is inferred from other species. [45]
Cost and whereClinic-administered only; needs an intravenous catheter and kidney bloodwork first. Generic zoledronic acid is about $147 per 4 mg vial, which is twenty cat doses, so the drug cost per visit is small if the clinic splits vials; the visit itself is typically $150 to $400. Older reports of over $1,000 per dose predate generics. [56]

Palliative radiation

Three or four weekly sessions, each under a short anesthesia, to shrink the tumor and ease symptoms. About half of cats get relief and median survival is two to four months. It is more involved than the measures above and is covered on the Radiation page. [1, 8]

Cost and whereSpecialty or university hospitals only. Published 2026 cost surveys put a palliative course of three to six sessions at $1,000 to $3,000, with specialty networks quoting up to $5,000; sessions are $200 to $500 each, plus a planning CT of $800 to $2,500 and a consultation of $150 to $300. [57, 58]

Surgery

Surgery offers the longest survival when the tumor can be removed with clean margins, mainly at the front of the lower jaw. Tongue, throat and back-of-mouth tumors are rarely removable.

  • Mandibulectomy (part of the lower jaw removed), 21 cats with SCC. Median 217 days; 43% alive at one and two years; front-of-jaw tumors 911 days; 38% recurrence. Afterwards 72% had trouble eating at first, 12% never ate on their own again and 41% needed a feeding tube; 83% of owners were satisfied. [6]
  • Radical mandibulectomy (75–90% of the jaw), 8 cats. Mean 712 days; 3 recurrences; 6 of 8 ate on their own. [2]
  • Maxillectomy (part of the upper jaw removed), 60 cats at 14 hospitals, 13 of them with SCC. Across all 60, median survival was not reached, 18% had local recurrence, and the commonest complications were poor appetite (20%, median 7 days) and wound breakdown (20%). For the 13 SCC cats, 83% were alive at one and two years; 2 of 13 had incomplete removal and 1 died of recurrence. Selection matters: these were tumors a surgeon judged removable, often small (median 2 cm), and 8 of the 60 cats also received chemotherapy. [36]
  • Total glosso-mandibulectomy (the whole lower jaw and tongue removed), 20 cats in Japan, 2008 to 2022. Median survival 533 days; 50% alive at one year and 38% at two; the tumor stayed away for a median of 914 days. Complications in 18 of 20 cats, including airway swelling that needed a breathing tube in three. Every cat needed a permanent stomach feeding tube and daily clearing of saliva from the mouth at home. [63]

Across these series the clean margin is what matters. After glosso-mandibulectomy, margin status was the only factor tied to survival [63]; after mandibulectomy the tumor came back in 9 of 16 cats with incomplete margins against 4 of 17 with clean ones [6]. A head CT before surgery and a planned bone margin of about 1 cm matter more than the name of the procedure. The 83% owner satisfaction after mandibulectomy comes from the same series in which 98% of cats had early complications and 12% never ate again on their own; both numbers belong together [6].

Clinical trials

Trials listed as open on institution or registry pages as of October 2026, nearest to Oregon first. None has been confirmed by phone, and trial pages go stale, so call the coordinator before any travel. Most trials need the biopsy result, a CT scan and bloodwork from a referring oncologist first. All are single-site academic studies, and every visit is at the sponsoring hospital.

Intratumoral immunotherapy (LTN), Colorado State University, Fort Collins

What it isLipid Toll-like-receptor nanoparticles (LTN), an agent that switches on the innate immune system, injected directly into the tumor. Based on two trials in horses with SCC that CSU reports as highly successful; tested in only one cat with oral SCC before this. [22, 37]
ProtocolFour injections under anesthesia, one every three weeks. CT scans and biopsies before treatment, at the third injection and three weeks after the fourth. Five visits and five anesthesias in all. Stopping tumor growth counts as success. [22, 37]
Who can joinConfirmed oral SCC of the gum, tongue, lip or jaw bone that a needle can reach; no spread beyond local lymph nodes; good liver and kidney function; fit for repeated anesthesia. Excluded: ear or nose tumors, advanced kidney disease, liver disease, diabetes, immune-suppressing drugs. [22, 37]
Cats plannedNot published. [22, 37]
Cost to ownerThe owner pays for the tests that diagnose the cancer and any other tests the oncologist recommends. Coverage of the injections and scans is not stated; ask when calling. CSU expects families to live within about 150 miles or relocate for the study. [22, 37]
Results so farNone. Expected side effects are swelling at the injection site and a day of tiredness and reduced appetite. [22, 37]
Status and contactRecruiting 16 August 2026 to 30 December 2027. csuoncologytrials@colostate.edu, (970) 297-4001. Investigator Steve Dow. Registry VCT26005965. [22, 37]

What it isThree consecutive daily sessions of stereotactic radiation plus two tablets taken at home: olaparib, which blocks a DNA-repair enzyme (PARP) so irradiated cells cannot mend themselves, and bexagliflozin, a feline diabetes drug that starves cells of glucose. Funded by the Morris Animal Foundation ($196,166). [70, 30]
ProtocolRadiation once every 24 hours for three days, oral drugs at home, a feeding tube placed in every cat, CT scans and samples of tumor and lymph nodes at set points. [70, 30]

Excluded: diabetes, untreated other illness, poor anesthetic risk, kidney disease at IRIS stage 2 or worse, raised liver enzyme (ALT above 107). Screening needs bloodwork, urinalysis, thyroid test and a head CT. [70, 30]
Cats planned18. [70, 30]

Frunevetmab (Solensia) with meloxicam and palliative radiation, University of Pennsylvania, Philadelphia

What it isThe only randomized, placebo-controlled study ever designed for this disease. Every cat gets meloxicam and six sessions of palliative radiation; half also get frunevetmab, an antibody against nerve growth factor licensed for arthritis pain in cats, and half get a saline placebo. The question is pain relief, not survival. [71]
ProtocolSix visits over 56 days, including the six radiation sessions under anesthesia, weekly owner questionnaires, and a head CT at the end. [71]
Who can joinOral SCC of the lower or upper jaw confirmed by cytology or biopsy, with bone destruction visible on CT; no spread to lungs or lymph nodes; a cat that tolerates handling. Excluded: chemotherapy, immunotherapy, anti-nerve-growth-factor drugs or radiation within the previous three weeks; heart, lung or kidney disease that rules out anesthesia or meloxicam. [71]
Cats plannedNot stated. [71]
Cost to ownerThe study pays for all six radiation treatments with anesthesia and exams, the antibody or placebo, meloxicam, monitoring bloodwork, chest X-rays and the final CT. The owner pays for the initial staging: head CT, bloodwork and the tumor sample. [71]
Results so farNone. [71]

Radiation plus camonsertib, NC State, Raleigh

What it isThree palliative radiation sessions (8 Gy each, 72 hours apart) for all cats; some also get camonsertib, a drug that blocks ATR, an enzyme tumors use to repair radiation damage. The design mirrors a pending human head and neck cancer trial. [24, 73]
Who can joinBiopsy-confirmed oral SCC anywhere in the mouth, otherwise healthy, no prior chemotherapy or radiation. [24, 73]
Cats plannedSix, three per arm. [24, 73]
Cost to ownerNothing; all testing and treatment provided, about $8,500 value. [24, 73]
Results so farPresented at the ASTRO meeting in September 2025: safe dose set at 5 mg/kg under the skin in three healthy cats; five of six trial cats enrolled; one cat whose tumor grew on radiation alone then received radiation plus camonsertib off-trial and had a complete response lasting three months at the time of the report. Side effects were reversible anemia, low white cells, raised liver enzyme and fever. [24, 73]
Status and contactNearly full in September 2025 and no longer on NC State's trial page; probably closed. Dr. Effie Palyvou, epalyvo@ncsu.edu. [24, 73]

Personalized RNA vaccine, University of Florida, Gainesville

What it isMessenger RNA copied from the cat's own tumor, wrapped in lipid nanoparticles and infused into a vein, so the immune system learns to attack the tumor. The same platform produced strong immune responses against brain tumors in 10 dogs and 4 people. [14, 27, 74]
ProtocolThree 30-minute intravenous infusions over seven weeks, then rechecks at two weeks, one month and three months after the last. [14, 27, 74]
Who can joinAny cat with a diagnosis of oral SCC and no other major illness. Surgery, radiation or chemotherapy may be given alongside as standard care. [14, 27, 74]
Cats plannedNot published. UF lists the study as "limited enrollment". [14, 27, 74]
Cost to ownerThe study pays for the three vaccine visits, bloodwork, immune monitoring and the three rechecks. The owner pays for the initial evaluation and diagnostics and for any standard treatment. [14, 27, 74]
Results so farNone published in cats. A related UF trial of an LSD1 inhibitor tablet after radiation shows "enrollment paused". [14, 27, 74]
Status and contactListed as recruiting, October 2026; referral through an oncologist. Inquiry form on the trial page, (352) 294-4389. Principal investigator Dr. Carlos Souza. [14, 27, 74]

Porphysome photodynamic therapy, Ontario Veterinary College, Guelph, Canada

What it isLight-activated nanoparticles infused into a vein that gather in the tumor. A camera shows where they are, then laser light through an optical fiber activates them to kill tumor cells while sparing healthy tissue. Developed with human head and neck surgeons at Toronto's Princess Margaret Cancer Centre; funded by the Canadian Cancer Society. [75]

Cats plannedNot published. [75]

Experimental treatments

Treatments that have been tested in cats, or in cat tumor cells, but are not available in regular practice. They are ordered from most to least evidence. Each entry gives the number of cats, how the cats were chosen, what was found and what it would cost an owner today.

STAT3 decoy (CS3D)

What it isA small loop of DNA that mimics the sequence the STAT3 protein binds, soaking it up. STAT3 drives tumor growth and suppresses immunity and has resisted drugs for decades; the target sequence is identical in cats and humans. In mice the effect depended on CD8 T-cells, so it works partly through the immune system. [17]
StudyPhase 1 at UC Davis with UCSF, published in Cancer Cell, November 2025. [17]
Cats20 client-owned cats with biopsy-confirmed oral SCC (tonsil tumors excluded), at least one year old and 2 kg, tumor at least 10 mm; median tumor 2.3 cm (range 1.1 to 8.7). No chemotherapy, radiation or bisphosphonates during the study; NSAIDs and pain medicines allowed. [17]
ProtocolSix intravenous doses over 22 days (days 1, 3, 5, 8, 15, 22). Three cats each at 2.5, 5 and 7.5 mg/kg and 11 cats at 10 mg/kg. Study period 29 days. [17]
ResultsTwo partial responses, five stable, thirteen progressed: a 35% disease-control rate with no relation to dose. Median survival 72.5 days overall (range 7 to 316); the seven responders lived a median of 161 days versus 57 for the rest (P = 0.004), though survival was not a primary endpoint. [17]
Side effectsNo dose-limiting toxicity. Mild anemia in 12 cats (7 grade 1, 5 grade 2), none needing treatment. One responder had skin ulceration over the shrinking tumor that healed in two weeks. [17]
What predicted responseA lower neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio in the blood, more immune-activation genes in the tumor at baseline, and a rise in PD-1 by day 8. Unexpectedly, responders had less activated STAT3 in their tumors at the start. [17]
Availability and costNot available outside a trial. Further cat and human trials are planned; no open cat enrollment was found as of October 2026. Two authors co-invented the drug and hold interests in the licensee. [17]

IB-DNQ with weekly radiation

What it isA drug activated by the enzyme NQO1, which is high in these tumors and rises further after radiation; once activated it floods tumor cells with reactive oxygen. [31, 32]
StudyProspective trial at the University of Illinois, 2018 to 2021, funded by the Morris Animal Foundation. [31, 32]
Cats18 cats with confirmed oral SCC, screened by bloodwork and a CT scan before entry. [31, 32]
Protocol8 Gy of radiation once a week for four weeks, with 1 mg/kg IB-DNQ given within four hours of each session. Tumor size measured by CT before and after. [31, 32]
Results17% partial response and 78% stable disease on CT, a 94% biological response rate. Responses tracked with the amount of NQO1 in the tumor. Survival was not reported. [31, 32]
Side effectsNo significant blood or organ toxicity. In the pilot, mild stomach upset and a short rise in breathing rate. [31, 32]
Availability and costThe trial covered treatment after the initial evaluation. No follow-on trial was found; the drug is licensed to a company and not available in practice. [31, 32]

Radiation with zoledronate

What it isA bisphosphonate that blocks the bone-eating cells the tumor recruits, given with weekly radiation for tumors that have invaded the jaw. [25]
StudySingle-arm trial at the University of Illinois, published 2022. [25]
Cats9 cats with oral SCC invading bone (3 lower jaw, 6 upper jaw) and no spread to lymph nodes or chest. Eight finished; one was withdrawn for heart failure. [25]
Protocol8 Gy of radiation once a week for four weeks and 0.2 mg/kg zoledronate by 15-minute infusion on days 0 and 22, with a third dose at day 50. [25]
ResultsBy tumor volume, 44% partial response, 44% stable and 11% progressed; no complete responses. Median survival 136 days (range 41 to 200). The blood marker of bone breakdown fell in every cat. [25]
Side effectsAll radiation effects were grade 1 (mild); kidney values did not change; a brief, symptomless dip in calcium and phosphorus. [25]
Availability and costBoth parts are available in practice. Zoledronate is sold in vials sized for a 40 kg dog, so the cost per cat depends on whether the clinic can split vials; weekly palliative radiation is priced by the clinic. [25]

Electrochemotherapy

What it isElectric pulses delivered through needle electrodes open pores in tumor cells so that bleomycin, given intravenously, floods in. It works on tumors the electrodes can reach. [18, 21]
StudyRoutine for skin SCC in Europe. Skin SCC: 23 cats, 2025. Mouth: one tongue case report, 2026. [18, 21]
CatsSkin series: 23 cats, 19 with tumors on the nose; early-stage (T1 and T2) tumors did best. Mouth: one 14-year-old cat with a tongue tumor. [18, 21]
ResultsSkin: 78% response rate, 27% recurrence, 466 days before recurrence. Mouth: after three sessions the tumor shrank, pain eased, the cat ate on its own and gained 0.9 kg; autopsy later found deep spread under the surface. [18, 21]
Availability and costOffered at some referral centers, mainly in Europe; each session needs anesthesia. Treated as palliative for the mouth. [18, 21]

Not yet tested in cats

  • EGFR blockers (cetuximab, gefitinib) slowed growth in cell lines, but 2026 DNA sequencing found the EGFR gene amplified in only 6% of tumors, so they are unlikely to work alone. [2, 20]
  • PI3K/mTOR inhibitors are proposed from the same sequencing (PTEN lost in 44% of tumors), ideally paired with carboplatin or radiation. [20]
  • Checkpoint immunotherapy (anti-PD-1 or anti-PD-L1), which transformed human head and neck cancer, is at the reagent stage for cats: PD-L1 is present on feline SCC samples and anti-feline PD-1 antibodies exist, but none has been given to a tumor-bearing cat. The mutation burden that predicts response is low (mean 0.7 per megabase in the 2026 series of 36 tumors; 3.7 in an earlier series of six), so single-agent checkpoint therapy is not expected to work well. An earlier LAK-cell immunotherapy was safe but did not extend survival. [2, 20, 76, 77]
  • Light-activated therapies: EGFR-targeting nanobody photodynamic therapy killed feline SCC cell lines (Utrecht, 2021); porphysome photodynamic therapy is funded at Guelph. No cat has been treated yet. [33, 34]
  • Lactate-transport blockers: tumor cells feed on lactic acid from neighboring cells; this was confirmed in cat tumors, but the drug is not available for cats. [35]
  • Tried and abandoned: umbilical-cord stem cells (survival under a month), liposomal cisplatin (no responses) and ethanol ablation (transient shrinkage). [2]

Recent data, 2025–2026

  • February 2025. Total glosso-mandibulectomy in 20 cats, Japan: median 533 days, 50% alive at one year, at the cost of lifelong tube feeding. [63]
  • April 2025. Radiation plus carboplatin, 47 cats: median 164 days. Adding toceranib afterwards made no significant difference (208 versus 162 days), except tongue tumors regrew later. [3]
  • July 2025. Electrochemotherapy for skin SCC, 23 cats: 78% response, early-stage best. [18]
  • October 2025. Review of the field in Frontiers in Veterinary Science. [2]
  • September 2025. NC State reported early results of camonsertib with radiation at the ASTRO meeting: safe at 5 mg/kg, five of six cats enrolled, one off-trial complete response. [73]
  • November 2025. STAT3 decoy phase 1 in 20 cats, Cancer Cell: 35% disease control; responders lived 161 versus 57 days; mild anemia only. The first targeted drug with measurable benefit here. [17]
  • January 2026. 4,300 biopsy cases in Germany: 41% of cat SCC in the mouth, 36% on skin. [19]
  • February 2026. DNA sequencing of 36 tumors: TP53 mutated in 71%, PTEN lost in 44%, MYC gained in 41%, EGFR gained in only 6%. The tumors resemble human HPV-negative head and neck cancer. EGFR drugs alone look unlikely to work; PI3K/mTOR drugs with radiation are suggested; checkpoint drugs still worth testing. [20]
  • 2026. Electrochemotherapy for a tongue tumor, one cat: shrank and the cat ate again; deep spread found at autopsy. [21]

Access from Oregon

What an owner in the Willamette Valley can reach, as of October 2026.

Oregon State University, Corvallis

The Carlson College of Veterinary Medicine's teaching hospital has a board-certified oncology service offering surgery, chemotherapy and advanced diagnostics; oncology 541-737-4812. Its clinical-trials page lists no feline or oral-tumor trial. The hospital does not offer radiation: its own fundraising page says the teaching hospital "does not offer radiation treatment, yet" and is raising money for a linear accelerator. Ask the oncology service directly whether that has changed. [78, 79]

Nearest radiation

VCA Northwest Veterinary Specialists in Clackamas, south of Portland (16756 SE 82nd Drive; 503-656-3999), offers intensity-modulated, stereotactic and palliative radiation for cats, led by a radiation oncologist. Referral comes through the primary veterinarian. Washington State University in Pullman, which developed the accelerated radiation-plus-carboplatin protocol with the best-documented outcomes, also treats feline oral SCC. [80, 5]

Nearest trial

No treatment trial for this cancer exists west of Colorado. The closest is Colorado State's immunotherapy study in Fort Collins, about 1,200 miles by road from Corvallis, and CSU expects families to live within about 150 miles or relocate for the study period. Every other trial requires repeated visits at the sponsoring hospital in Illinois, Pennsylvania, North Carolina, Florida, Missouri or Ontario. [22, 37]

Before any travel

Trial pages stay online after enrollment closes (the NC State camonsertib trial, the Illinois flyers and the Florida LSD1 page are examples), so a phone call to the coordinator is the only reliable check. Most trials want the tumor sample result, a CT and bloodwork from a referring oncologist first. Current listings are at the AVMA Veterinary Clinical Trials Registry, veterinaryclinicaltrials.org.
